# First principles thinking

```text
- First principles thinking breaks a problem into basic truths, then rebuilds the answer.
- It replaces "how is this normally done?" with "what is actually true?"
- Most thinking is by analogy: copy an existing solution and make small changes.
- The method: state the goal, list assumptions, test each one, keep the verified parts, rebuild.
- Use it for novel or costly problems; for routine choices, analogy is faster.
```

First principles thinking is a way to solve problems from the ground up. You strip away inherited assumptions. You keep only what you can verify. Then you build a new answer.

The idea is old. Aristotle called a first principle "the first basis from which a thing is known." In modern use, a first principle is a basic fact or law that you cannot break down further.

## Two ways to think

|          | Reasoning by analogy        | Reasoning from first principles  |
| -------- | --------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Start    | An existing solution        | The goal and basic facts         |
| Move     | Copy and adjust             | Break down and rebuild           |
| Result   | Small improvements          | New options                      |
| Best for | Familiar, low-risk problems | Novel, costly, or stuck problems |

Most daily thinking is analogy. It is fast and usually good enough. First principles thinking is slower. Use it when the default answer looks expensive, arbitrary, or impossible.

## How to do it

1. Write the goal in one sentence.
2. List what you believe about the problem.
3. Mark each belief as a fact, a law, or a convention.
4. Break the problem into parts you can measure or verify.
5. Rebuild a solution from the verified parts only.
6. Test the result against reality.

The hard step is step 3. A convention can feel like a fact. "This takes three days" may be a queue, not a law. "This costs this much" may be a price, not a cost.

## Worked example: a slow report

A monthly report takes three days. The team assumes it must take three days.

Break it down:

- Pull the data: 2 hours.
- Clean the data: 1 hour.
- Analyze it: 2 hours.
- Wait for review: 2 days.
- Format it: 1 hour.

The actual work is about 6 hours. Most of the delay is waiting for review. That is the fundamental fact.

Rebuild:

- Automate the data pull.
- Book the review before the report is due.
- Use a fixed template for formatting.

The report can now finish in about one day. The team did not work faster. It removed a queue that looked like work.

## Famous example: rockets

Elon Musk's often-cited example is the rocket. Instead of accepting the market price, he asked what a rocket is made of. The materials are aluminum, titanium, copper, carbon fiber, and fuel. He estimated that the raw materials were about 2% of the typical rocket price. The exact number is his estimate. The useful part is the question: which costs are physical, and which are convention?

## Limits

- **It is slow.** It costs more mental energy than analogy.
- **It needs knowledge.** You must know which facts are real in that domain.
- **False fundamentals are dangerous.** If you mistake an assumption for a fact, you rebuild on sand.
- **Shallow breakdown fails.** Stop too early and you miss real costs or constraints.
- **It is not always needed.** For routine work, use the proven pattern.
